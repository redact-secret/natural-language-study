# URD-003: Without capitalization, Urdu person names that are also common nouns depend on local context

## Question and scope

Which Urdu given names and name parts are also ordinary words, and what context do existing Urdu NER sources use to separate the readings? Scope: Urdu in Pakistan, `Arab` script (with the sources' own Roman transliteration, `ur-Latn`, where they use it), news and general text as reflected in the cited datasets, PERSON. Excluded: person versus place or organization collisions (noted as a gap), measured prevalence, other Pakistani languages.

## Observation

Arabic script has no letter case, and Urdu NER papers name this as a main difficulty. One lists "no capitalization" first among its issues for Urdu NER ([URD-S005](../sources.md#urd-s005), pp. 2509-2510). Another states that the lack of capitalization removes a major clue and makes disambiguation harder than in English ([URD-S006](../sources.md#urd-s006), section IV, items 1 and 10). Dataset slides repeat lack of capitalization and complex orthography as reasons Urdu NER is hard ([URD-S007](../sources.md#urd-s007), slide 7).

Both NER papers name person/common-noun collisions. [URD-S005](../sources.md#urd-s005) (p. 2510) gives barkat (blessing) and salamat (safety) as words that can be a person name or a common noun, and reports an unresolved case, phatah (victory, also a name), where its title, designation, surname and postposition checks all fail (p. 2516, limitation 7); it also says postpositions sometimes accompany common nouns, so the postposition cue is not decisive. [URD-S006](../sources.md#urd-s006) (item 10) lists Shan, Kamran, Fazal, Kiran, Aftab and Manzoor as words that can be PERSON or common nouns, and contrasts a sentence in its own Roman transliteration where "fazal" is "blessing" (not an entity) with one where it is a student's name (PERSON) (section V).

The IJCNLP-08 Urdu data has 277 Person entities in about 40 thousand words, and the UNER set has 1207 Person entities among 4621 entities in 48,673 words ([URD-S007](../sources.md#urd-s007), slides 13 and 18-19). These are dataset sizes; they do not give the share of ambiguous names.

Confidence: **medium**. Two papers agree on the phenomenon and examples, but both are from related research circles, rely on small data, and none quantifies ambiguity.

## NER hypothesis

For Urdu strings that are both names and common words, a recognizer that relies on surface form without local context will mislabel the common-noun reading as PERSON (or the reverse); adding context features such as a following agent or object postposition, a preceding title or designation, or a following predicate will reduce errors on matched minimal pairs but will not remove them, and the postposition feature alone will over-trigger on common nouns (example-06). The hypothesis fails if surface-only and contextual models perform alike on matched pairs, or if the postposition feature does not over-trigger.

## Evidence handoff

Positive cases: fictional names from the lists in the sources used as names in matched sentences. Hard negatives: the same strings as common nouns in matched sentences (examples 01-05), plus common nouns with `نے` or `کو` (example-06). Variation axes: sentence-initial versus mid-sentence, with or without title, with or without postposition, ambiguity class (place and organization collisions are a gap). Destination: `ner-evidence`; deferred pending native-speaker review of the reading of each example.

## Evaluation handoff

Proposed slice **urdu-name-common-noun-minimal-pairs**: hold the string fixed and vary context; report false positives on the common-noun reading and misses on the name reading separately. Destination: `ner-eval`; deferred.

## Implementation options

Gazetteer-only baseline versus gazetteer plus context rules (as in [URD-S005](../sources.md#urd-s005)) versus contextual model features. Tradeoff: a gazetteer list increases the ambiguity problem rather than solving it. Destination: `fastner`; deferred, nothing executed.

## Limitations and review

No ambiguity prevalence; no quantitative comparison across the cited systems; person/place and person/organization collisions (for example a name used as a place) not studied. The synthetic examples rely on author judgment of Urdu usage; no qualified reviewer checked them.
