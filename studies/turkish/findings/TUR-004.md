# TUR-004: ASCII-fied Turkish (diacritic loss) hides name identity and creates collisions with common words

## Question and scope

When `ç ğ ı ö ş ü İ` are replaced by `c g i o s u I` (typing without a Turkish keyboard, legacy systems), what happens to personal names and to name/common-word distinctions? Scope: Latin-script Turkish; informal and constructed text; PERSON. Excludes measured prevalence and specific platforms.

## Observation

Turkish "deasciification" (restoring diacritics) is a recognized task: Tür's Bilkent thesis lists text deasciification among the information-extraction tasks it addresses, alongside name tagging [TUR-S007](../sources.md#tur-s007); only the repository abstract was read. Küçük and Steinberger list diacritics-based expansion of lexical resources among the changes tested for Turkish tweet NER [TUR-S010](../sources.md#tur-s010), again from an abstract. Together these show that diacritic loss was treated as a practical problem in NER-adjacent work; they do not give prevalence or effect sizes.

Collisions are inferable from the dictionary: after ASCII folding, `ışık` (light) and `Işık` the name both become `isik`, and `ipek` (silk) [TUR-S014](../sources.md#tur-s014) differs from the name `İpek` only by the capital dotted letter that ASCII folding removes. Whether a particular ASCII form is a name, a common noun or both is context-dependent. The dictionary shows `gökçe` as a common adjective sense (blue, sky-coloured); that it is also a given name is not supported by the entries read.

Confidence **low**: sources are abstracts; collisions are the author's inference from dictionary entries.

## NER hypothesis

Diacritic loss reduces recall of dictionary- and gazetteer-based name recognition, and deasciification (or a fold-insensitive match) before recognition recovers part of it without adding person/common-noun errors. Challenge: no recall loss on matched ASCII-fied inputs, or recovery that is offset by false positives on common words.
