# HIN-004: Anusvara, chandrabindu, half-consonant and joiner variation changes name strings without changing the name; danda sticks to the last word

## Question and scope

Which Devanagari conventions produce several valid or common strings for one Hindi name, and how does sentence-final danda interact with a name at the end of a sentence? Scope: Hindi, hi-IN, Devanagari, edited prose; PERSON. Excludes rendering, fonts, other languages written in Devanagari, and prevalence. The question matters because string-matching, lexicon lookup and token boundaries can diverge on invisible or alternate-form differences.

## Observation

The Central Hindi Directorate standard keeps both anusvara and chandrabindu in Hindi, says they can distinguish meaning, and prescribes anusvara for a nasal consonant followed by a consonant of its own class (examples include पंकज, गंगा, चंचल, संपादक), with chandrabindu used in native (tadbhava and deshaj) words, replaced by a bare bindu when a vowel sign sits above the headline [HIN-S001](../sources.md#hin-s001), sections 2.1.1 and 3.6, printed p. 9 and 28-30 (decoded from an extraction with font artifacts). The same source says writers often confuse the two and offers a pair that differs only by chandrabindu (हंस and हँस). So alternate spellings of a name exist in principle (anusvara versus a written half-nasal conjunct such as पङ्कज; bindu versus chandrabindu), and the standard prescribes one form without recording how often the others occur.

Unicode treats U+0901 candrabindu and U+0902 anusvara as distinct combining signs, U+094D virama as the sign that suppresses the inherent vowel and forms conjuncts, and ZERO WIDTH NON-JOINER and ZERO WIDTH JOINER after a virama as controls that block a conjunct or request a half form [HIN-S002](../sources.md#hin-s002), section 12.1 (fetch summary). These are invisible code points that can sit inside a rendered name. Local computation confirms that NFC and NFD leave U+0901, U+0902, U+094D, U+200C and U+200D untouched, so normalization does not unify these variants [HIN-S004](../sources.md#hin-s004). Danda U+0964 is the sentence-ending sign, and the standard says it is the only full-stop sign adopted from the Indian tradition [HIN-S001](../sources.md#hin-s001), section 3.16.2, printed p. 34. The standard's section 2.2 says official-purpose numerals are the international form of Indian numerals, with Devanagari digits U+0966..U+096F permitted additionally on authorization.

Confidence is **medium**: official prescription plus encoding facts verified locally; no attested variant counts.

## NER hypothesis

A tokenizer that splits punctuation at U+0964 and drops U+200C/U+200D from comparison keys (while keeping them in the original text) will reduce spurious boundary and lexicon-miss errors on names with these variants without changing recognition of unrelated words; variant sets must be produced from reviewed text, not from this list. Challenged if the model already yields equal embeddings for the variants, or if stripping joiners merges distinct names or distinct words (the standard's हंस/हँस pair shows bindu forms can differ in meaning for common words).

## Evidence handoff

Positive cases: each variant type in names; sentence-final names with danda. Hard negatives: common words that differ only by bindu versus chandrabindu; names with conjuncts. Variation axes: bindu/chandrabindu, conjunct, joiner, digit script. Destination: `ner-evidence`; deferred.

## Evaluation handoff

Proposed slice `hi-diacritic-variants`: equal names in variant forms with unchanged annotation; boundary error versus recall. Also `hi-danda-final`. Failure condition: no difference to baseline. Destination: `ner-eval`; deferred.

## Implementation options

Baseline: raw text. Option: generate matching keys that fold U+200C/U+200D and (separately) U+0901/U+0902, never rewriting stored text. Tradeoff: folding bindu/chandrabindu loses a real contrast. Destination: `fastner`; deferred.

## Limitations and review

How often each variant occurs in names is not measured; the source is a prescriptive standard for official Hindi. ZWNJ/ZWJ behavior in real corpora (copy-paste, keyboard layouts) was not studied. Rendering differences across fonts were not examined. No reviewer.
