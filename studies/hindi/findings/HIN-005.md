# HIN-005: Devanagari offers no capitalization cue, and Hindi names collide with common words in the same script

## Question and scope

Without case, which cues distinguish a Hindi personal name from a homographic common word, and what do existing resources record about this? Scope: Hindi in Devanagari (hi-IN) with a contrast to Roman-script Hindi (hi-Latn); PERSON versus common noun or adjective; no claim about other Indian languages. Excludes person/place or person/organization ambiguity except where noted.

## Observation

HiNER's authors list absence of capitalization, ambiguity (the female name Pushpa as proper noun and as common noun "flower"), spelling variation and free word order as the challenges for Hindi NER, and say there is no camel case for names either [HIN-S005](../sources.md#hin-s005), sections 1 and 3. The IJCNLP shared-task tutorial slides likewise list absence of capitalization as a major missing clue and illustrate with a romanized sentence in which the word aanand (a possible name, here the noun bliss) is not a named entity, and say proper nouns are not always named entities in context [HIN-S007](../sources.md#hin-s007). By contrast, the Roman-script tweet corpus uses a capitalization feature, noting that people usually capitalize names in Roman script [HIN-S008](../sources.md#hin-s008), features section. That is an author statement about typical writing, with no measurement of how often the capital is absent in tweets.

In HiNER's error analysis, the majority of errors tag a named entity as non-entity, followed by boundary confusion between B-Person and I-Person [HIN-S005](../sources.md#hin-s005), section 5. The paper reports a confusion matrix, not an ambiguity-specific study, and the error types are not attributed to homography.

Confidence is **medium** for the existence and relevance of the problem (two annotation projects state it; the tweet corpus only assumes capitalization in Roman), **low** for any quantitative claim, because no source reports homograph error rates.

## NER hypothesis

A name lexicon feature without context will fire on common-noun uses of these words, and local context (following surname, ने/को after a human-agent verb, predicate agreement) will reduce but not remove the errors; performance gaps will be larger for the Devanagari ambiguity slice than for the capitalized Roman slice. Challenged if the model already separates ex-01/ex-02 from context, or if Roman lowercase text loses as much as Devanagari. This concerns classification, not span edges.

## Evidence handoff

Positive cases: ambiguous word as person in several syntactic frames. Hard negatives: same string as flower, sea, hope, ray, adjective. Matched pairs, one word per pair. Variation axes: following surname present or absent, postposition present, script, case in Roman. Destination: `ner-evidence`; deferred, no issue opened. Uncertain contexts must be kept and labeled instead of forced.

## Evaluation handoff

Proposed slice `hi-name-homograph`: matched person versus common-word contexts; report false positives on the common reading and misses on the person reading separately. Destination: `ner-eval`; deferred.

## Implementation options

Baseline: contextual encoder only. Option: lexicon features with explicit common-noun counter-evidence. Tradeoff: a lexicon alone cannot resolve the pair in ex-07. Destination: `fastner`; deferred.

## Limitations and review

All Hindi examples are author-written with no qualified review; some pairs may sound unnatural to a native speaker. No homograph frequency source was found. HiNER's own annotation of ambiguous items was not examined at example level because extraction garbled its Devanagari. The Roman capitalization claim is the paper's assertion only.
