# HIN-006: Romanized Hindi names have no single mapping from Devanagari, and code-mixed text keeps Hindi grammar words around Latin names

## Question and scope

How do Devanagari and Latin renderings of the same Hindi name relate, and where do Hindi function words sit when a name is written in Latin script inside Hindi-English text? Scope: hi-IN Devanagari and hi-Latn romanization, informal Hinglish and library romanization; PERSON. Excludes transliteration identity linking, other Indian scripts, and other languages. Transliteration does not establish identity.

## Observation

The Library of Congress ALA-LC Hindi table (2025 version) supplies the inherent vowel a after consonants unless a vowel sign or virama blocks it, renders anusvara by a nasal chosen from the next consonant or by m, renders anunasika as n̐ or m̐, and brackets the nukta letters as used for Urdu words [HIN-S010](../sources.md#hin-s010), Notes 2-5. In the notes read, there is no word-final schwa deletion rule. Spoken Hindi does delete orthographic schwas, and predicting where is a non-trivial task even for a statistical classifier on a pronunciation lexicon, which is why a letter-by-letter Roman rendering often differs from how people write names informally [HIN-S011](../sources.md#hin-s011), abstract and Introduction. The two sources together support only that a cataloguing scheme and a pronunciation-oriented spelling can disagree; neither measures informal romanization of names.

In attested code-mixed tweets, the corpus authors annotate Roman-script names with their following Hindi grammar words as separate tokens, for example `modi/B-Per ji/I-Per na/Other` and `media/B-Org ko/Other`, and they removed Devanagari-only and English-only tweets, so the corpus is Roman-script Hindi-English only [HIN-S008](../sources.md#hin-s008), sections 3 and 3.1. A hashtag error example splits `#Modi` and `gi`. A 2025 Hindi-English code-mixed dataset describes outputs in standard English, Romanized Hindi and Devanagari Hindi and a schema with Person plus hashtag, mention and emoji types [HIN-S009](../sources.md#hin-s009), abstract and task description. These sources show that both scripts and mixed forms are research-relevant; they do not establish what share of Hindi text uses each.

Confidence is **medium** for the existence of the mapping gap (official table plus a published schwa study) and for the code-mixed annotation pattern (two corpora); **low** for how many romanizations a given name has, because no attested variant counts were found.

## NER hypothesis

A model or lexicon that sees only one script or one romanization will miss the other script's rendering of the same fictional names, and a script-change-as-boundary cue will wrongly split ex-05 and rightly split ex-04. Hypotheses concern recall on cross-script name variants and boundary behavior at script changes. Challenged if a multilingual encoder matches the cross-script pairs without help, or if script change does not correlate with edges in real data. No claim is made about identity linking.

## Evidence handoff

Positive cases: matched Devanagari and Roman names in the same templates; Roman names with Devanagari postpositions; script-internal switches. Hard negatives: Roman English common words in Hindi sentences; names that look like English words. Variation axes: capitalization, schwa-final spelling, ji/ne/ko spacing. Destination: `ner-evidence`; deferred. Variant sets must come from reviewed text.

## Evaluation handoff

Proposed slice `hi-cross-script-names`: identical names in Devanagari, Roman-capitalized, Roman-lowercase and mixed forms; report recall by script and boundary errors separately. Destination: `ner-eval`; deferred.

## Implementation options

Baseline: no transliteration. Option: generate Roman variants from a reviewed set for lexicon matching; option: script-aware tokenization that does not treat script change as a hard edge. Tradeoff: a deterministic romanizer may produce strings nobody writes. Destination: `fastner`; deferred.

## Limitations and review

ISO 15919, IAST and ITRANS were not read at primary sources. The code-mixed sources are social-media corpora of one era and domain; COMI-LINGUA was read only in part. Whether Hinglish speakers write names in Devanagari or Latin in a given genre is not measured here. No reviewer.
