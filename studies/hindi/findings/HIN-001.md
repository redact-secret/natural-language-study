# HIN-001: Standard Hindi writes case postpositions after names as separate words but fuses them to pronouns

## Question and scope

Where does a PERSON span end before a case postposition (ने, को, से, में, पर, का/की/के) in contemporary standard written Hindi (hi-IN, Devanagari), and does the answer differ for personal pronouns? Scope: edited prose and official-style orthography; romanized Hindi is touched only through one attested code-mixed tweet corpus. PERSON only. This finding is about Hindi alone. The attachment behavior of Marathi, Bengali and other Indian languages is not examined and nothing here is claimed about them; see [the dossier scope](../overview.md). The question matters because a whitespace-delimited word that contains both a name and a postposition would force a within-token boundary, while a separate word does not.

## Observation

The Central Hindi Directorate's spelling standard states that Hindi postpositions are written separately from nouns of all kinds, with राम ने, राम को, राम से, राम का as its illustrations, and that postpositions are joined to pronouns, with तूने, आपने, तुमसे, उसने, उसको, उससे, मुझको, मुझसे as illustrations. When a pronoun takes two postpositions, the first is joined and the second is separate (उसके लिए). When ही or तक intervenes between pronoun and postposition, the postposition is separate [HIN-S001](../sources.md#hin-s001), section 3.2.1 and 3.2.2, printed p. 26-27. The examples were decoded from an extraction of the PDF with font artifacts (see the source record).

The UD Hindi HDTB page, as summarized by the fetch tool, lists ने, को, का and की as separate ADP tokens in a news treebank [HIN-S006](../sources.md#hin-s006). In an attested Roman-script code-mixed tweet corpus, the guideline example annotates `modi/B-Per ji/I-Per na/Other` and `media/B-Org ko/Other`, so the ergative marker and the accusative/dative marker are separate tokens outside the entity [HIN-S008](../sources.md#hin-s008), section 3.1. These two corpus sources describe annotation of text that already has the spacing; neither measures how often real text omits the space.

Confidence is **medium**: one normative standard plus two annotation sources (the UD page was read only as a summary), no usage-rate evidence, and no grammar (Kachru or McGregor) was accessible for this pass. The observation is scoped to the standard written norm.

## NER hypothesis

For edited standard Hindi, a whitespace token boundary after the last name word will coincide with the PERSON span end before a following case postposition in most cases, so a model with a Hindi-specific suffix-stripping rule will gain little over whitespace candidates on a standard-orthography slice and may lose on pronoun controls. This is a prediction about boundary behavior, not classification. It would be challenged if a spacing-noisy slice (ex-04 style) shows frequent fusion in realistic text, or if name-final strings are mistaken for postpositions. Whether noisy fusion occurs in attested social text is open.

## Evidence handoff

Positive cases: names (given plus surname) followed by each of ने, को, से, में, पर, का, की, के. Hard negatives: pronoun plus ने/को; ordinary nouns plus the same markers. Contrast families: standard spacing versus deliberately glued variants labeled synthetic noise. Variation axes: genre, Devanagari versus Roman, hyphen/spacing. Destination: `ner-evidence`; deferred, no issue opened.

## Evaluation handoff

Proposed slice `hi-postposition-spacing`: hold model, split and annotation policy fixed; compare whitespace candidates against any proposed suffix-aware candidates; count boundary errors separately from missed entities, with pronoun controls reported separately. Question, not a measured result. Destination: `ner-eval`; deferred until reviewed evidence exists.

## Implementation options

No-change baseline (whitespace plus Devanagari-aware tokenization); optional pronoun-suffix guard so ने/को/से fused to pronouns are not treated as name boundaries; character-level handling only for the noisy-glued slice. Tradeoff: a general suffix list helps nothing in standard orthography and can damage names that end in these sequences. Destination: `fastner`; deferred.

## Limitations and review

No Hindi grammar was accessible, so the oblique and vocative stem changes of names (for example masculine names ending in -आ, vocative forms) are not covered here; they are listed in [morphology](../morphology.md) as unsourced leads. UD and HiNER-style annotation choices are not linguistic authorities. No reviewer; author self-check only. Other Indian languages are out of scope.
