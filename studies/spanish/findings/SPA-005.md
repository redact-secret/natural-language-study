# SPA-005: Capitals carry accents and Unicode forms vary, so canonical normalization is separate from accent loss

## Question and scope

How should accented Spanish names (á é í ó ú ü ñ) be matched across precomposed, decomposed and accent-stripped forms? Scope: Latn Spanish names in edited and noisy text; PERSON. Excludes transliteration and other scripts.

## Observation

RAE guidance reportedly states capital letters take the tilde whenever accentuation rules require it ([SPA-S004](../sources.md#spa-s004); excerpt only). Unicode defines precomposed characters and combining sequences as canonically equivalent, with NFC and NFD as the two normal forms ([SPA-S012](../sources.md#spa-s012), Normalization Forms). Applied here (author application, not source text): `é` U+00E9 equals `e` plus U+0301 under normalization, and `ñ` U+00F1 equals `n` plus U+0303. Dropping the accent (García to Garcia) is not a canonical equivalence; it is a lossy transformation, and the result can collide with a different name or word. How often accents are lost in real Spanish text is not established here.

Confidence **medium**: the normative rule and the Unicode equivalence are documented; the accent-loss behavior is a constructed stress test.

## Illustrative examples and contrasts

All **synthetic**, fictional people. NFD rows contain the combining marks named in the Meaning column; text preserved as written.

| example-04 | `Nuñez, Núñez y Nunez.` | Three spellings of one imagined surname. | Accent and tilde variants; do not pool as one identity. |
| example-05 | `¿Dónde está Lucía? ¡Lucía!` | Where is Lucía? Lucía! | Inverted punctuation adjacent to a name; boundary should exclude the marks. Unsourced stress case. |

## NER hypothesis

Hypothesis (untested): NFC-normalizing input for lookup while preserving original offsets recovers the NFD condition (example-02) without effect on example-03; accent loss needs a separate treatment. Refuted if the normalized lookup also changes example-04 results or damages offsets.
