# GRE-001: Greek names inflect for case, so the surface mention often differs from the lemma

## Question and scope

How much does case inflection change the surface form of a Greek personal name relative to its nominative (dictionary) form? Scope: Modern Greek, Grek script, edited prose, given names and surnames of Greek form, entity type PERSON. Excludes dialects, Cypriot Greek, plural names and, except as a contrast, foreign-origin names. It matters because a gazetteer or feature keyed on nominative forms can miss genitive, accusative and vocative mentions.

## Observation

UD Greek documents four cases for nouns and says dative survives only in fixed expressions ([GRE-S001](../sources.md#gre-s001), Morphology; summary read). A Modern Greek grammar overview gives masculine -ος nouns as -ος / -ου / -ο / -ε (nominative / genitive / accusative / vocative) and says stress can move forward in the genitive of longer words ([GRE-S008](../sources.md#gre-s008); secondary, summary read). Wiktionary lists Γιώργος, Γιώργου, Γιώργο for nominative, genitive, accusative, with the vocative also Γιώργο, and for the surname Παπαδόπουλος a genitive in two stress variants (Παπαδόπουλου, Παπαδοπούλου) and vocative Παπαδόπουλε ([GRE-S010](../sources.md#gre-s010); community-edited). The feminine Ελένη has one form for nominative, accusative and vocative and the genitive Ελένης ([GRE-S010](../sources.md#gre-s010)).

Disagreement within the sources read: the grammar overview shows vocative -ε for masculine -ος nouns, while Wiktionary lists the given name Γιώργος with vocative Γιώργο. Read together, vocative form for names is lexically or stylistically variable; this dossier does not settle the distribution.

Author application, not source text: Νίκος (Νίκου, Νίκο) and -ίδης surnames such as Μαυρίδης (genitive, accusative and vocative Μαυρίδη) follow the same classes; these were not individually checked in a source.

Confidence **medium**: the case system is documented by UD, but name-specific forms rest on secondary or community-edited pages and no primary grammar text was accessed.

## NER hypothesis

Hypothesis (untested): features or gazetteers keyed on stem plus case-ending families, or on character n-grams, recover genitive, accusative and vocative person mentions that a nominative-only lookup misses, without a net rise in false positives on common nouns that share endings (example-07). It is refuted if nominative-only lookup matches the stem-aware condition on held-out inflected mentions, or if the extra false positives offset the recovered mentions. The claim concerns surface mentions, not linking to a lemma identity.

## Evidence handoff

Positive cases: one fictional person in four cases; both masculine and feminine names; surnames in -ος, -ης. Hard negatives: common nouns with -ου/-ο/-ε endings. Variation axes: gender, name class, stress-shifted genitive, formal -ε versus familiar -ο vocative. Destination `ner-evidence`; deferred pending qualified Greek review of examples and an annotation-policy decision. No issue opened.

## Limitations and review

Name-specific inflection rests on Wikipedia and Wiktionary summaries; plural names, diminutives such as Γιωργάκης, Cypriot forms and indeclinable names are not researched. No corpus frequencies are given. No qualified Greek-language review is recorded; the author's self-check of examples is not independent review.
