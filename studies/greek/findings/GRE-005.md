# GRE-005: Greek names appear in official, informal and look-alike Latin forms, so transliteration is not identity

## Question and scope

How do Greek names appear in Latin letters (official transliteration, informal Greeklish, and look-alike Greek and Latin capitals), and what does that imply for recognizing and matching them? Scope: Modern Greek names in Grek script with Latn transliteration contrasts; passports and identity documents, informal messaging, and constructed homoglyph stress cases; PERSON. Excludes machine-transliteration quality, Greek in other scripts and phonetic rendering rules beyond the sources. It matters because one person can appear as Παπαδόπουλος, PAPADOPOULOS and a differently spelled Latin form.

## Observation

A secondary page reports that Greece uses ELOT 743 for romanization on passports and identity documents, that ELOT 743 (approved 1982, revised 2001) and ISO 843 are essentially equivalent, and that a 2011 legal decision permits alternative spellings if the standard form also appears ([GRE-S005](../sources.md#gre-s005); secondary, summary read). The ELOT catalogue record shows ΕΛΟΤ 743.0 was published in 1993, withdrawn in 2001 and replaced by ΕΛΟΤ 743 Ε2:2001, with ISO 843 as equivalent ([GRE-S013](../sources.md#gre-s013); metadata only; the standard text was not read). A 2003 Data Protection Authority decision, as summarized, ruled that new documents must not transcribe a name differently from how the person had already appeared in records, citing accuracy of personal data ([GRE-S014](../sources.md#gre-s014); point numbers unverified). Together these indicate that the official Latin form is a standard-governed rendering and that people can hold a different, document-consistent Latin spelling.

Greeklish, informal Latin writing of Greek, is described in an LREC 2006 abstract as not standardized, with competing conventions based on shape, sound or keyboard position ([GRE-S011](../sources.md#gre-s011); abstract only). The secondary page gives digit-for-letter examples such as 8elo for θέλω ([GRE-S005](../sources.md#gre-s005)).

Look-alike letters (author application, verified by script): many Greek capitals are distinct code points from Latin letters that look identical, for example Greek Α U+0391 and Latin A U+0041, Greek Ο U+039F and Latin O U+004F, Greek Β U+0392 and Latin B U+0042 ([GRE-S003](../sources.md#gre-s003) for the Greek code points). Mixed-script look-alike strings are a recognized spoofing and extraction risk in general, but no source here measures their frequency in Greek.

Confidence **low** to **medium**: the existence of ELOT 743 and Greeklish is documented, but the standard text was not read, the Greeklish evidence is one abstract, and the rest rests on secondary summaries.

## NER hypothesis

Hypothesis (untested): a Greek-script PERSON recognizer degrades on Latin-script and Greeklish renderings of the same names, and on mixed-script look-alike tokens, unless trained or augmented with them; script-aware normalization helps look-alikes but not Greeklish. It is refuted if recall on Latin-script transliterations matches Greek-script recall for the same names, or if homoglyph normalization has no effect on matched mixed-script items. The claim does not assert that Latin and Greek forms refer to one identity.

## Evidence handoff

Positive: Greek-script name, ELOT-style Latin form, alternative Latin form, code-switched sentence, all-caps variants. Hard negatives: common words in Latin letters, homoglyph strings that spell a different word. Variation axes: document register (passport-style versus chat), casing, accent loss. ## Implementation options

Candidate experiments: script-mixing flag feature, Unicode confusables folding for detection only, transliteration augmentation. Baseline is no-change. Do not use transliteration to merge identities. Destination `fastner`; options only, nothing adopted.

## Limitations and review
